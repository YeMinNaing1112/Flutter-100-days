# Problem Space — In The Circle

> **Status:** Early discovery — hypotheses only, not validated.

## Original idea

The original GitHub note describes the idea as being for long-distance relationships, but does not yet explain the specific problem or intended outcome.

## Initial problem hypotheses

From the founder's perspective, maintaining a healthy long-distance relationship can be difficult because of:

- **Physical distance:** Partners cannot be together in person as often as they would like.
- **Communication breakdown:** Keeping communication consistent can be challenging.
- **Trust and insecurity:** Distance may contribute to insecurity and misunderstandings.
- **Limited shared experiences:** Partners have fewer opportunities to share everyday experiences in person.
- **Commitment and emotional support:** Maintaining the relationship takes effort from both partners and requires emotional support.
- **Busy and different schedules:** When partners have different busy schedules, finding ways to maintain a healthy relationship can be hard.

These are initial ideas based on the founder's perspective. They are not yet validated findings about other couples.

## Current discovery checkpoint

The founder's current working direction is that In The Circle should help couples stay involved in each other's everyday lives while supporting their personal goals and making intentional time for their relationship. It should not be limited to long-distance couples; it may also apply to couples living in the same city or together.

### Intended outcome

The founder wants both partners to:

- Support each other's personal goals while making time for their relationship.
- Share everyday experiences voluntarily.
- Take shared responsibility for maintaining the relationship.
- Make plans together and follow through on them.
- Stay involved in each other's lives without needing to be available to each other all day.

### Target couple hypothesis

Couples who genuinely want their relationship to work but struggle to maintain connection while managing busy lives and personal goals. The working assumption is that both partners care about the relationship, but struggle to consistently turn that care into everyday actions.

### Emerging problem hypothesis

The founder selected these possible obstacles as especially important:

- **Scheduling and availability:** Their responsibilities and schedules make it difficult to find suitable time to connect.
- **Lack of balance:** Work, study, and personal goals can take so much time and energy that the relationship receives less attention.
- **Communication and sharing:** Partners may not consistently share their everyday experiences or understand what's happening in each other's lives.
- **Intentions without plans:** Partners may want to spend time together but not turn that intention into concrete plans.
- **Unequal participation:** One partner may end up initiating, sharing, or planning more often, making the effort feel one-sided.

The founder currently sees scheduling and availability as the bigger obstacle compared with simply forgetting to connect. This is a personal working hypothesis, not a validated finding.

### Important distinction

The founder chose the scenario where partners have opportunities to connect but don't make meaningful use of them as less central than the scenario where they struggle to fit connection around their responsibilities. The current question is whether knowing each other's schedules would be enough, or whether couples would also need to share experiences and make intentional plans.

Schedule awareness may be one part of the problem, but it has not been established as the root cause or solution.

## Open question to resume with

**If both partners know each other's schedules, is that enough to help them feel more connected?**

- **A. Yes:** Knowing when each partner is busy or available helps them understand each other and coordinate time.
- **B. Partly:** Schedule awareness helps, but partners also need to share everyday experiences and make intentional plans.
- **C. No:** Schedules are not the main issue; partners need to change how they participate in each other's lives.

Resume discovery by answering this question and explaining why. Do not move into feature design yet.

## Discovery principles

- Understand the problem before proposing features or solutions.
- Treat personal experience as a starting point, not proof that all couples share the same problem.
- Later, investigate whether other couples experience the same difficulty and how often it happens.
- Distinguish the underlying problem from its consequences and possible solutions.
- Do not treat this early problem space as a final product vision or validated solution hypothesis.
